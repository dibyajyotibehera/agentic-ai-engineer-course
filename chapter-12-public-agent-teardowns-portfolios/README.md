# Chapter 12 — Public Agent Teardowns & Portfolios

*The capstone. Everything in Chapters 1–11 was preparation for this: you take a real agent you have built, you open it up in public, and you defend it the way an experienced engineer defends a design in review — with the decisions written down, the cost of every task accounted for, the latency broken down step by step, and the results measured against a baseline you stated before you ran anything.*

## The idea in plain words

A teardown is an engineering argument made in public, not a demo. A demo shows what an agent can do when everything goes right, and it hides everything else; a teardown shows what the agent actually does, what it costs, where it spends its time, where it fails, and why it was built the way it was, and it invites the reader to check every one of those claims. That difference is the whole point of this chapter, because a demo is forgotten the moment the video ends, whereas a teardown is evidence, and evidence is what survives a design review and your own memory six months later when the system breaks and you have to remember why you chose the shape you chose.

The worked example throughout this chapter is the support copilot you have been building across the course: a [ReAct](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) loop — a loop in which the model reasons, acts through a tool, observes the result, and reasons again — that resolves support tickets by reading a knowledge base, checking account state, issuing refunds, and changing subscriptions, escalating to a human when it cannot resolve the ticket safely. Four [Model Context Protocol](https://github.com/microsoft/ai-agents-for-beginners) tools fan out per ticket — account state, subscription status, knowledge-base search, and refund eligibility — and [LLM-as-a-judge](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith) evaluations, in which a second model grades the agent's work against a rubric, act as the production gate, so no new version of the copilot ships unless the judge says it is at least as good as the version already running. If you have built a different agent of your own, use that one instead; the shape of the teardown is the same either way, and the capstone exercise at the end of this chapter asks you to produce exactly this document for your own system.

Why publish the teardown at all, rather than keeping the same analysis in a private design document? Because a private document is only ever read by people who already trust you, while a public teardown is read by people who do not, and that is precisely the audience that matters: a future employer deciding whether you can be trusted with a production system, a peer deciding whether to adopt your pattern, and your future self deciding whether the trade-offs you made still hold. The discipline of writing for strangers is what makes the analysis honest, since every claim you publish has to survive a reader who can run your benchmark, re-price your cost table, and point out the failure mode you left out. That is also why this chapter is the capstone rather than an afterthought: the teardown is where the techniques of the previous eleven chapters stop being exercises and start being a single coherent story about one system, and the story is only as strong as its weakest measurement.

## The mechanism, part one: writing the decision down

Every teardown starts with the decisions, because cost and latency numbers are meaningless until the reader knows what was decided and what was rejected. The instrument for this is the architecture decision record, usually shortened to ADR, a short document that captures one important architectural decision together with its context and its consequences, in the format set out in [Documenting Architecture Decisions](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions) (2011), which [this template collection](https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/architecture-decision-record) has since made the standard starting point. The rule that matters most for a teardown: an architecture decision record is written as a conversation with a future developer, in full sentences, and it records the forces that were in tension, the options that were considered, the decision taken in the active voice, and all of the consequences, positive and negative alike, because a decision whose downsides are hidden is a decision nobody can revisit honestly.

For an agent, the record needs three fields that the original template does not have, and the template in `code/adr_template.md` adds them: the effect on evaluation, meaning whether the decision makes the agent easier or harder to grade; the effect on cost per task, meaning whether it adds model calls, tool calls, or tokens to every ticket; and the effect on latency, meaning whether it adds a sequential step to the critical path or runs in parallel with something else. Those three fields are what turn an architecture decision record from a history document into a teardown document, because they connect each decision directly to the numbers in the next two sections, and a reader who disagrees with a decision can see exactly what it costs to reverse it. The support copilot's first record, included as a worked example in `code/adr-001-react-loop-over-dag.md`, records the choice of a free-form ReAct loop over a fixed [directed acyclic graph](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents) of steps: the loop was chosen because ticket shapes are unpredictable and the model needs to decide for itself whether to check the knowledge base before or after the account state, and the price of that flexibility is recorded honestly alongside it, namely that an unbounded loop can run away, which is why the same record also records the stopping rules — a maximum step count, a token budget, and a hard stop on repeated tool calls — that keep the flexibility from becoming a cost incident. That is the pattern for the whole teardown: every decision is stated, every rejected alternative is named, and every cost is admitted in the same document.

The records also do something quieter, which is that they make the teardown cumulative. When a later decision reverses an earlier one, the old record is not deleted or edited; it is marked superseded, with a pointer to the record that replaced it, so the reader can follow the reasoning as it changed. A portfolio that shows one reversal, explained well, is more convincing than a portfolio that pretends the first design was right all along, because real systems are revised and a hiring panel knows it.

## The mechanism, part two: accounting for every task

Once the decisions are written down, the next question a sceptical reader asks is what the agent costs, and the only honest answer is a cost per task, not a monthly bill. A monthly bill hides the shape of the spending — whether ten tickets cost ten times one ticket, or whether one bad ticket cost as much as a hundred good ones — while a cost-per-task breakdown shows the price of each step, each tool call, and each evaluation, and it lets the reader scale the number to their own volume. The method is simple, and it is borrowed from the [Claude Cookbook](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-cookbooks) cost-optimisation recipe, which works through a checklist on a real agent, measuring pass rate and cost per task together so that the cheapest configuration is only accepted if it still clears the quality bar; a configuration that is half the price and fails twice as often is not cheaper, it is worse, and the teardown has to show both numbers side by side to make that visible.

The accounting itself has four lines, and the calculator in `code/cost_per_task.py` implements exactly these four. First, the model cost of the agent loop, which is the input and output tokens of every model call in the run multiplied by the provider's published per-token prices, including the tokens spent on tool definitions, since a tool-heavy agent pays for its tools on every call whether it uses them or not. Second, the tool cost, which is usually zero for internal tools but is real for paid application programming interfaces, and which has to be counted per call, not per task, because a loop that retries a failing tool five times pays five times. Third, the memory cost, which is the price of the context the agent carries: the three-tier memory system from Chapter 4 is not free, since working memory is re-sent on every call, episodic memory has to be retrieved and summarised, and semantic memory has to be maintained, and the teardown prices each tier so the reader can see whether the memory is earning its keep. Fourth, and most often forgotten, the evaluation cost, which is the price of grading the task at all, and which is where the most striking verified number in this chapter comes from: in a [LangChain study](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith) published in September 2026, the decision model [Jev](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith) from TypeSafe AI matched a human reviewer's pass-or-fail labels on all 500 repeated decisions across five captured agent runs, at a total cost of $0.34, while [Claude Sonnet 4.6](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith) acting as a judge matched the human labels on only 80.0% of the same decisions and cost $28.17 for the same run, which is about 83 times more expensive for a less reliable verdict. That study is narrow — five cases from a single weather agent, judged 100 times each, with the language-model judges running at provider defaults — and the teardown must say so, but it still establishes the point that matters for the copilot: the judge is a line item in the cost of every task, and choosing a cheaper, more repeatable judge can be the difference between evaluating every ticket and evaluating a sample, which is the difference between a gate that catches regressions and a gate that misses them.

The same accounting exposes the cost of the fallback paths, and the computer-use arithmetic from Chapter 3 is the sharpest example of why that matters. Declaring the computer-use toolset adds about 4,500 input tokens to every request, each screenshot costs roughly 1,000 to 1,800 input tokens, and a 30-step task comes to about 783,000 input tokens, or about $1.61 on [Claude Sonnet 5.5](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool) at list prices, according to [Anthropic's computer-use documentation](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/computer-use-tool). That figure belongs in the teardown not because the copilot drives a screen on every ticket, but because the copilot's fallback for a broken internal tool is sometimes a screen-driven one, and a fallback that costs $1.61 a task is a fallback that needs a budget and a kill-switch, which is exactly the Chapter 11 argument, now priced. The rule that ties the section together is that every number in the cost table traces to a source: a provider price page, a measured trace, or a cited study, and any figure that is an assumption is labelled as one, because an invented price in a public teardown is not a rounding error, it is a credibility failure.

## The mechanism, part two-and-a-half: seeing the cost while it happens

A cost table in a teardown is a photograph of one run, and a photograph is not enough once the agent is serving real traffic, because the bill that arrives at the end of the month is the sum of thousands of runs nobody watched individually, and by the time it surprises you the money is already spent. Cost observability is the step that turns the four-line accounting above from a document you write after the fact into a signal you watch while the agent runs, and it starts with the same discipline: every model call must carry the facts that let you price it later, which means the model name, the input and output token counts, and the cost, recorded on the call itself rather than reconstructed from a log line afterwards. The shape to copy is the one standardised by the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions, which are the agreed names for telemetry from model calls, agents, and tools: one agent run becomes a root span for the agent invocation, beneath it sits one chat span per model call, named after the model that was called, and one tool-execution span per tool call, and each span carries the attributes that price it, the requested and responding model names, the input and output token counts, and the operation and provider names that say whose meter is running. The conventions are young and still marked as in development, and they have recently moved house into their own repository, so attribute names can still change between releases and the practical rule is to pin the version of the conventions next to the version of your instrumentation library and to treat message content as opt-in, recording metadata by default and never capturing ticket text or tool arguments unless you have decided, deliberately, that the debugging value is worth the privacy risk. The design-review angle is the same as for the cost table, only live: when a reviewer asks how you would know the agent was getting expensive before the invoice arrived, the answer is that every call is already a priced span, and the dashboard is a query over spans, not a guess over logs.

Once the spans exist, the next decision is who the cost belongs to, and it has to be made before the bill arrives, because a monthly invoice tells you what the whole product spent and nothing about which user, which team, or which feature spent it. The attribution hierarchy runs from the smallest unit upward — per call, then per task, then per user or team, then per product — and each level is a rollup of the one below it, so a task's cost is the sum of its calls' four lines, a conversation's cost is the sum of its tasks, and a team's cost is the sum of its conversations, and the calculator in `code/cost_per_task.py` now implements exactly that rollup alongside the original four-line calculator, attributing each step's cost to its task and rolling tasks up to a per-conversation total. The reason the hierarchy has to be decided up front is that attribution is a labelling decision made at the moment the span is emitted: the span needs the task identifier, the conversation identifier, and the owner or team tag attached to it when it is created, and once spans have been aggregated without those tags there is no way to split them back apart, so the team that discovers it needs per-team costs in the month the bill spikes is the team that needed to tag its spans in the month before. A teardown that shows the hierarchy, with one conversation priced end to end, is making a claim a reader can test, which is that the agent's economics are understood at the level the business actually thinks in, and that claim is what the unit economics below are for.

The third thing observability has to show is not the total but the multipliers, because most cost incidents are not a single expensive call, they are a small cost repeated until it is large. The first amplifier is the tool schema: every model call re-sends the definitions of every tool the agent may call, whether it uses them or not, so adding a fifth tool to the copilot does not cost one tool call, it costs a little more on every call the agent ever makes, and the computer-use arithmetic from the previous section is the extreme form of the same mechanism, with about 4,500 input tokens of toolset definition attached to every request before a single screenshot is taken. The second amplifier is retrieval in context: every document the agent pulls from the knowledge base is re-sent on every subsequent call in the loop, so a ticket that retrieves three long documents and then loops five times pays for those documents five times, and the fix is the same shape as the memory pricing in the four-line accounting, summarise what was retrieved, keep only what the next step needs, and price the context you carry. The third amplifier is multi-agent fan-out: the copilot's four tools already fan out per ticket, and when a design fans out further, to a sub-agent per tool or a critic per draft, each branch pays its own model calls and its own context, so a fan-out that halves latency can easily triple cost, and the teardown has to show both numbers, the way the latency section shows both sequential and parallel time. None of these is a reason not to use tools, retrieval, or fan-out; each is a reason to measure the multiplier before you scale it, because a multiplier that is invisible at ten tickets a day is the whole bill at ten thousand.

That brings the section to the unit the business actually understands, which is the cost per conversation, or per task, as a single number with a margin story attached to it. The worked example below is measured, not invented: running `code/cost_per_task.py` against three live tickets on 22 November 2026, priced at Moonshot's list prices for Kimi K3.1 Pro ($2.00 input / $10.00 output per million tokens), gives a mean cost of $0.0199 per ticket and a worst ticket of $0.0222, and the two-ticket conversation the attribution rollup prices comes to $0.0415, so at the assumed volume of 10,000 tickets a month — an assumption, stated — the copilot costs $199.46 a month. The router actually billed $0.0064 for the nine model calls in that run, well below the list-price table, because the judge runs on the cheaper `:floor` route and router prices sit below list, so the list-price table is an upper bound and the teardown should print both. The arithmetic is deliberately simple. The point of the example is the question it lets you ask next: whatever a conversation is worth to the business, in support hours saved or in revenue retained, the agent is worth running only while the cost per conversation stays below that value with room to spare, and the moment the cost per conversation moves — a model swap, a new tool, a longer context — the teardown's unit number is the tripwire that catches it, because a 10% rise in cost per task is a 10% rise in the monthly bill at fixed volume, and nobody needs a finance meeting to see it. Explain it back this way: the four-line table prices one task, the spans price every task as it happens, the hierarchy says whose task it was, the amplifiers say why the price moved, and the unit economics say whether the price is worth paying — and if any one of those five is missing, the cost story in the teardown is incomplete.

## A second teardown subject: the harness itself

The cost teardown answers one question, which is whether the agent is worth running, and it leaves a second question unanswered, which is why the agent behaves the way it does. Two agents built on the same model can cost the same per task and still behave nothing alike, because the model is only one part of the system, and the rest of it is the harness. A harness is the program that sits around the model: the loop that sends the conversation to the model, runs the tools the model asks for, and pastes the results back, together with everything that decides what the model is allowed to see and do. The model reasons, and the harness is what turns that reasoning into action in the world, which is why a teardown of the harness belongs beside the teardown of the unit economics. The cost teardown tells you whether the agent is worth running, and the harness teardown tells you why it behaves the way it does.

A harness teardown examines five subsystems, and the frame is the same for every harness, which is what makes different harnesses comparable. First, instructions, which is the standing text the agent reads before it starts work: the system prompt, the project rules, and the documents the harness loads into context on every run. For the support copilot, the instructions are the system prompt plus the refund policy the agent reads from the knowledge base. Second, state, which is what the agent remembers from one step to the next: the working context of the current run, the summaries of past runs, and the memory tiers from Chapter 4, and the question to ask is what survives when the context window fills or the run restarts. Third, verification, which is how the harness checks the work before it counts: the tests the agent must run, the judge that grades the draft, and the human approval gate on the refund, and the question is what stops a wrong action from reaching the user. Fourth, scope, which is what the agent is allowed to touch: which tools it may call, which files it may change, and which actions are fenced off entirely, so a read-only tool can never issue a refund however the model phrases the call. Fifth, lifecycle, which is how a run starts, how it ends, and what happens in between: the stopping rules that cap the loop, the retries, the timeouts, the handoffs between agents, and the cleanup when a run finishes. Across all five, the teardown asks one question in three forms: what does the harness let the agent see, what does it let the agent touch, and what does it let the agent do, and the answers are written down as decisions, with the rejected alternatives named, in the same architecture decision record format as the rest of the teardown. For the support copilot, the same five questions explain behaviour the cost table cannot: why the copilot escalates instead of refunding is a scope decision, why it forgets a ticket's history is a state decision, and why it stops after a fixed number of steps is a lifecycle decision.

Four production harnesses show how different the answers can be, and each one is defined by a single design decision. The first is Pi, whose defining decision is to keep the core minimal and make every capability a programmable extension. The harness ships as a small core with extension points, and new behaviour arrives by writing an extension rather than by changing the core, so the same core can grow into very different agents. What it trades away is completeness out of the box: a fresh installation does little until extensions are added, and the behaviour of the finished agent depends on which extensions are installed and how they interact, which means two installations of the same core can behave like different agents. The second is Claude Code, whose defining decision is to treat context as a layered system the harness manages, rather than as a single transcript the model carries. Memory is organised in four layers with different lifetimes; compaction squeezes the transcript in five levels, each level trading detail for space; hooks run code at defined moments in a run, such as before a tool call or after a file edit; and sub-agents work in isolation, so the context a sub-agent gathers never pollutes the main run. What it trades away is transparency: the more the harness manages behind the scenes, the harder it is for the operator to say exactly what the model saw on any given call, and every layer is another moving part that can surprise you when it triggers at the wrong moment. The third is Codex, whose defining decision is to make the repository the source of truth and let the harness read it before it acts. A short AGENTS.md file works as an index, a table of contents that points at deeper documents in the repository rather than trying to contain them, and each task runs in its own git worktree, an isolated copy of the repository, so one task cannot interfere with another. What it trades away is everything outside the repository: from the agent's point of view, knowledge that lives in chat threads or in people's heads effectively does not exist, and the isolation that keeps tasks apart also means each task starts cold, with no shared memory of the one before it. The scale this buys is visible in [OpenAI's own account of the experiment](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/): about a million lines of code built over five months, with roughly 1,500 pull requests opened and merged, and single runs that regularly worked one task for more than six hours. The fourth is DeepSeek, whose defining decision is that everything is a plugin. Capability arrives as a plugin attached at a defined seam, and an event pipeline carries what happens from one plugin to the next, so the harness grows by adding plugins rather than by redesigning a core. What it trades away is the coherence of a single designed core: each seam is a place where two plugins can disagree about state, and the pipeline is only as reliable as its weakest plugin, so a failure in one plugin can travel down the pipeline before anything notices.

The reason this teardown belongs beside the unit-economics teardown is that the harness is where the cost comes from and where the behaviour comes from, and a cost table on its own cannot tell the two apart. [Anthropic's own comparison](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps) makes the point with one model and one prompt: asked to build a two-dimensional retro game maker, Claude Opus 4.5 running bare finished in 20 minutes for $9 with the game's core feature broken, while the same model inside a full harness, with a planner, a generator, and an evaluator working in stages, took 6 hours and cost $200 and produced a playable game. Those are the article's own measurements from a single experiment, not an independent benchmark, and the lesson they carry is that the model never changed and the harness was the whole difference, which means the $200 in the cost table is not a property of the model at all. It is the price of the harness's decisions: the planning stage, the verification stage, the context resets, and the retries, each of which bought reliability at a price the cost teardown records and the harness teardown explains. The same logic applies to benchmark scores. Claude Sonnet 4.5 was reported at 77.2% on [SWE-bench Verified](https://www.swebench.com/), the human-filtered set of 500 real software tasks, and the methodology note is the tell: the score was measured with a simple scaffold giving the model two tools, a shell and a file editor, which means the number is a score for a model inside a harness, however small, and a different harness around the same model would score differently. Read that way, the harness teardown is the missing half of the cost teardown: the cost teardown says whether the agent is worth running at its current price, and the harness teardown says which decisions set that price and which decisions set the behaviour, so a reader who wants the agent cheaper or better behaved knows exactly where to start. In the published teardown, the harness section sits beside the cost section, and it ends the way every section of the teardown ends, with the decisions written down: for each of the five subsystems, what the harness lets the agent see, touch, and do, what it forbids, and what would have to change for the decision to be revisited.

## Distillation as a cost lever

There is one more cost lever that belongs in this chapter, and it is the one that looks most like cheating until you see how it is built, which is distillation: taking a behaviour that currently needs a big, expensive model and teaching a small, cheap model to do just that behaviour, and then letting the small model do it instead. The behaviour worth distilling is almost never the whole agent, because the whole agent is too broad and too unpredictable for a small model to imitate faithfully; it is a narrow skill the agent repeats constantly, and for the copilot the cleanest candidate is the routing decision itself, the choice, made on every ticket, of which of the four tools to call first, or whether the ticket needs a refund check at all, which is a small classification decision that the frontier model currently makes as part of a long, expensive reasoning call. The training set is built from real traffic rather than invented examples: you take the big model's routing decisions on tickets the copilot has already handled, label each one with the route it chose and whether the ticket resolved, keep the decisions that resolved and drop the ones that did not, and that labelled set becomes the small model's lessons, so the student learns the teacher's behaviour on the exact distribution of tickets your users actually send, not on a textbook distribution. What it costs is the distillation run itself, which is the price of generating the labels and training the student, plus the evaluation run that proves the student is as good as the teacher on a held-out set of tickets, and both of those are one-off costs, which is the whole point: you pay once to build the student and then every ticket after that pays the student's price instead of the teacher's, so the lever only makes sense when the behaviour is repeated often enough for the per-ticket saving to repay the one-off build, and the teardown should show that payback arithmetic rather than asserting it.

The trap is staleness, and it is the reason a distilled model needs more governance than a prompted one, because a prompted model reads the current policy from its context on every call, while a distilled model carries the policy it was trained on inside its weights, frozen at the moment of training. When the refund policy changes — a new eligibility rule, a new escalation threshold — the prompted copilot picks the new rule up the next time the knowledge base is updated, but the distilled router keeps routing the old way, confidently and cheaply, until somebody retrains it, and the failure is silent, because the router is not wrong in a way that throws an error, it is wrong in a way that sends refundable tickets to the wrong queue. The fix is to tie the student to the same version stamp the retrieval system already uses: every policy document, every routing rule, and every trained student carries a version, and the deployment checks the stamp before it serves, so a policy change that bumps the stamp automatically marks the student stale and blocks it from serving until it is retrained on traffic labelled under the new policy. That is the explain-back for this section, and it is the one a reviewer will press on: your distilled router is cheaper and wrong after a policy change, so explain the retrain trigger — the version stamp, the check at serve time, and the retraining run on newly labelled traffic — and if you cannot explain the trigger, you do not have a distillation strategy, you have a time bomb with a lower bill.

## The mechanism, part three: where the time goes

Cost tells the reader what the agent spends; latency tells them what the user waits for, and the two are not the same problem, because a task can be cheap and slow, or expensive and fast, and the fix for one is often the opposite of the fix for the other. Latency profiling is the discipline of breaking one agent run into its steps and measuring each one, so that optimisation starts from evidence instead of from a guess about which step is slow, and the profiler in `code/latency_profiler.py` is built for exactly that: it wraps an agent run, records the wall-clock time of every step and every tool call, and prints a flame-graph-style breakdown, a text picture in which each step is a bar whose length is its share of the total time, so the dominant cost is visible at a glance. The [Claude Cookbook](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-cookbooks) latency-optimisation recipe works the same way, measuring time to first token, total task time, and accuracy together, and accepting the fastest configuration only if it still clears the quality bar, which is the same discipline as the cost recipe with a different axis.

The profiling habit that matters most is to measure the distribution, not the average. A support copilot whose mean resolution time is 8 seconds but whose 95th percentile is 45 seconds is not an 8-second agent; it is an agent that is fast for easy tickets and painfully slow for the tickets that matter, and the mean hides exactly the cases the teardown should be explaining. That is why the profiler reports the median, the 95th percentile, and the maximum alongside the mean, and why the serving thresholds from Chapter 11 are stated as percentiles: an alert fires when 95th-percentile latency rises above 2 seconds, when the error rate rises above 5%, or when memory usage rises above 90%. Those thresholds are the tripwires, and the profile is the evidence that shows which step crossed them, so the two belong together in the teardown: profiling the real copilot over three runs on 22 November 2026 gives a mean of 50.88 seconds, a median of 50.73 seconds, and a 95th percentile of 59.69 seconds, which trips the 2-second alert threshold by a wide margin, and the flame graph says why, because the draft-resolution model call is the slowest step at a mean of 26.24 seconds, about 52% of a typical run, with the plan call and the judge call taking most of the rest, while the four tools together take about 0.00 seconds, so the slowness is model latency, not tool latency, and the threshold says that slowness is now a production incident rather than an annoyance.

There is also a structural lesson hidden in the profile, which is that the four Model Context Protocol tools fanning out per ticket are the copilot's main latency lever in principle, but not in this run. Fan-out is parallelisation, and parallelisation trades cost for time: four tool calls made concurrently cost the same in tokens as four made sequentially, but finish in the time of the slowest one rather than the sum of all four, which is why the profile distinguishes sequential time from parallel time and why the teardown reports both, and the profiler can be run with `--sequential` to measure the difference directly. In this copilot the four tools are local lookups that take about 0.00 seconds each, so the fan-out as built saves nothing measurable — a sequential run of one ticket took 48.74 seconds against about 50 seconds per run in parallel — and the profile proves the latency lever is the model calls, not the tools. The failure mode to name is the one the profile would make visible against a real downstream service, which is that a fan-out is only as fast as its slowest member, so one slow tool — in production usually the refund-eligibility check, which depends on a billing service the copilot does not control — dominates the whole ticket, and the fix is either a timeout with a graceful fallback, which is Chapter 11 again, or a cache, which is Chapter 4. Either way, the teardown does not assert the fix worked; it re-profiles, and it publishes the before and after.

## The mechanism, part four: benchmarking in public, honestly

The last part of the teardown is the benchmark, and it is the part most often done badly, because a benchmark without a stated baseline is a advertisement, not a measurement. The model to copy is the [Hugging Face Agents Course](https://github.com/huggingface/agents-course) final project, in which the student builds an agent and evaluates it on a subset of the [GAIA benchmark](https://huggingface.co/spaces/gaia-benchmark/leaderboard) — a set of 466 questions that are conceptually simple for humans but require browsing, tool use, file handling, and multi-step reasoning — and needs a score of 30% or higher to earn the certificate, with results published on a [student leaderboard](https://huggingface.co/learn/agents-course/unit4/introduction). Three features of that design are worth copying directly: the benchmark is public and fixed, so anyone can re-run it; the pass mark is stated before the work starts, so it cannot be moved after the fact to flatter the result; and the leaderboard is public, so the score is comparable with everyone else's. Your teardown should do the same for the copilot, even though the copilot's benchmark is one you build yourself, because a support copilot cannot be scored on GAIA: you define a fixed set of tickets, you freeze them, you state the pass mark and the baseline before you run anything, and you publish the results with the harness, so a reader can reproduce them.

The baseline deserves care, because it is the comparison that gives the score meaning. For the copilot, the honest baselines are three, and the benchmark table in the capstone exercise asks for all of them: the previous version of the copilot, so the reader can see whether the new version is actually better; a simpler system, such as a single model call with retrieval and no loop, so the reader can see whether the agent machinery earns its cost; and a human baseline, such as the resolution rate and time of the support team on the same tickets, so the reader can see whether the agent is good enough to ship at all. Each baseline needs the same treatment as the agent itself — the same tickets, the same scoring rubric, the same judge — because a benchmark that scores the agent with a generous judge and the baseline with a strict one is not a comparison. And the table needs the caveats written next to the numbers, not buried in a footnote: how many tickets were in the set, how they were sampled, what the judge agreed with humans on, and what the benchmark does not cover, such as angry customers, tickets in a second language, or the refund cases that were excluded because they needed a human signature. A caveat does not weaken a benchmark; it is what makes it believable, and a benchmark with no caveats is the first sign that the author has not looked for the failure modes.

The rollout numbers from Chapter 11 belong in this section too, because a benchmark is a claim about a version, and the rollout is how that claim is tested against real traffic. The copilot ships behind a canary deployment, in which the new version serves 5% of traffic while the stable version serves the rest, and the verdict needs at least 2,000 canary requests before it is trusted; the new version is promoted only if its error rate is no more than the stable version's error rate multiplied by 1.2 plus 0.001, its 95th-percentile latency is no more than the stable version's multiplied by 1.15, its evaluation pass rate is no more than 0.02 below the stable version's, and its cost per request is no more than the stable version's multiplied by 1.10. Those four inequalities are the release gate, and publishing them in the teardown tells the reader that the benchmark was not the end of the story — it was the entry ticket, and production traffic had the final say.

## Operating at scale: the ways a good agent quietly breaks

A teardown proves the agent works once, on a fixed set of tickets, and that is necessary but not sufficient, because the agent that passes its benchmark on Monday can still fall over on Friday, not by failing outright but by degrading in ways that no single number catches. The framing below is adapted from the public outline of ByteByteGo's "Build with Claude Code" course, which names five pillars of agentic engineering, translated here into advanced-level practice rather than the specifics of any one coding tool, and it is worth working through because each failure mode below has a symptom you can see, a measurement that reveals it, and a fix that is cheap if you catch it early and expensive if you do not.

The first is context bloat, where the prompt grows until the agent is slow and confused. The symptom is a copilot that was sharp on short tickets and starts wandering on long ones, calling tools it has already called and asking questions the ticket already answered, and the measurement that reveals it is the input token count per call plotted against the step number of the run, because a healthy run carries roughly the same context on every call while a bloated one grows call by call as working memory, retrieved documents, and past tool results all accumulate, and the cost table from the earlier section shows the same growth as money, since every token carried is a token paid for. The fix is a context budget with teeth, the Chapter 4 discipline applied as a limit rather than a guideline: summarise episodic memory instead of replaying it, keep only the retrieved passages the next step needs, and drop tool results once their information has been extracted, and then re-profile, because a context that shrinks by half is usually a latency and a cost win at the same time.

The second is skill explosion, where too many tools and skills are available and the router cannot choose. The symptom is the agent picking the wrong tool, or picking the right tool after a long hesitation, or calling two tools that do the same job, and the measurement is the tool-selection error rate — how often the agent's first tool call is not the one the trajectory needed — together with the count of tools offered on each call, because the error rate climbs as the count climbs, and the tool-schema amplifier from the cost section means every extra tool also costs tokens on every call whether it is used or not. The fix is to shrink the surface the router sees: group tools behind a single dispatcher that chooses internally, hide tools that a given ticket type can never need, and write tool descriptions that state when the tool should not be used, not only when it should, and then grade tool selection as its own evaluation, because a router you do not grade is a router you do not control.

The third is the review bottleneck, where every output needs a human and throughput dies. The symptom is a queue of agent outputs waiting for approval that grows faster than the reviewers can clear it, so the agent is fast and the system is slow, and the measurement is the time an output spends waiting for review against the time the agent spent producing it, because when waiting dominates producing, adding a faster model changes nothing. The fix is to grade the review itself: route low-risk outputs, such as a draft reply that changes nothing, past review entirely, sample rather than reviewing every high-confidence output, and reserve full review for the irreversible actions, the refund and the subscription change, which is the Chapter 8 human-in-the-loop discipline applied as triage rather than as a blanket rule.

The fourth is friction points, the places the agent stalls and waits. The symptom is a run that spends most of its time not thinking but waiting, on a slow downstream service, on a human approval, on a tool that retries, and the measurement is the latency profile from the earlier section, read as a waiting-time breakdown rather than a compute breakdown, because the flame graph shows directly which span is waiting and for what. The fix depends on what is being waited on, and that is the point of measuring first: a slow service gets a timeout with a graceful fallback, a repeated approval gets batched or delegated, and a retrying tool gets a circuit breaker, and each fix is verified by re-profiling, since a fix that does not move the flame graph did not fix anything.

The fifth is evaluation gaps, the behaviours nobody grades. The symptom is an agent that passes its benchmark and still embarrasses itself in production, on angry customers, on tickets in a second language, on the refund cases the benchmark excluded, and the measurement is coverage: the fraction of real traffic shapes that appear in the evaluation set at all, counted by slicing production tickets into shapes and checking each shape against the benchmark, because a shape with no test is a shape with no grade. The fix is to grow the evaluation set from production, adding the shapes that actually arrived, and to keep the caveats from the benchmark section honest, since a benchmark that does not cover a shape should say so, and the shape should be added before the next release, not after the next incident.

And the sixth, which sits above the other five and is the one this course adds to the framing, is you as the bottleneck, where the engineer is the single reviewer and approver everything queues behind. The symptom is familiar to anyone who has run a production agent alone: every model swap, every prompt change, every canary verdict waits on one person, and the measurement is the time a change spends waiting for its author against the time it spent being built, because when waiting dominates building, the team's throughput is one person's calendar. The fix is the same instrumentation the chapter has been building all along, only turned inward: the architecture decision record so a teammate can approve a change without reconstructing your reasoning, the canary verdict thresholds so promotion is a calculation rather than a judgement call, and the cost and latency dashboards so anyone on call can see whether the agent is healthy, and the design-review angle is that an experienced engineer who can say how their own team stops depending on them is describing a system that scales, not just a system that works.

## A second way in: rescue a failing agent

Not every teardown starts from a healthy agent, and the most useful portfolio piece after a clean teardown is a rescue, which is why the exercises add a second capstone variant: you are handed a failing support copilot, with a trace bundle generated by `code/rescue_fixture.py`, whose cost per task has roughly tripled while its evaluation pass rate has barely moved, and you must diagnose it with the instruments from this chapter — the cost table, the latency profile, the benchmark, and the architecture decision records — and write the rescue plan as a set of records, each one naming the decision, the evidence, and the revisit trigger. The exercise is in `exercises.md` as Exercise 4, and it is deliberately harder than the capstone in one specific way: the capstone asks you to prove a system you built works, while the rescue asks you to prove, from someone else's traces, why a system that used to work stopped working, which is the skill an experienced engineer is actually hired for.

## The capstone brief

Here, then, is the brief, stated once and plainly: tear down the support copilot, or your own agent if you have one, in the same shape as this chapter, and publish it. The document has four parts, and each part uses a technique from an earlier chapter, so the teardown is also the revision. The decisions section is a set of architecture decision records, one per significant choice, written in the architecture decision record format with the three agent-specific fields, and it must include the ReAct loop against the directed acyclic graph, the four-tool fan-out, and the choice of judge; that is Chapter 1, Chapter 2, and Chapter 10, made concrete. The cost section is a cost-per-task breakdown built with the calculator in `code/cost_per_task.py`, with the four lines — model, tools, memory, evaluation — priced from your own traces and the judge arithmetic from the LangChain study, and with the computer-use fallback priced from Anthropic's token figures; that is Chapter 3, Chapter 4, and Chapter 10, priced. The latency section is a profile produced by `code/latency_profiler.py`, reporting the median, the 95th percentile, and the maximum, with a flame-graph-style breakdown that names the slowest step and the fix you applied, measured before and after; that is Chapter 5, Chapter 8, and Chapter 11, timed. And the benchmark section is a table against the three baselines — previous version, simpler system, human — with the pass mark stated in advance and the caveats written next to the numbers, followed by the canary verdict that shipped the version; that is Chapter 10 and Chapter 11, proven. Publish it as a public repository with the code, the traces, and the harness, so that a stranger can reproduce the numbers, and link it from your portfolio. That is the deliverable, and it is the whole course in one document.

## The advanced-level design-review angle

A public teardown is the strongest evidence you can bring to a design review, and the reason is structural rather than cosmetic. A resume claims that you shipped a production agent; a design review tests whether you can talk about it; a teardown proves both at once, because it shows the system, the numbers, and the reasoning, and it does so before the reviewer has asked a single question. When a reviewer asks you to describe a hard trade-off, you do not have to reconstruct it from memory under pressure — you point to the architecture decision record that records it, with the rejected alternatives and the consequences, and you walk through the reasoning that is already written down. When they ask how you think about cost, you show the cost-per-task table and the judge arithmetic, and you explain why you chose a cheaper judge rather than a more expensive one, which is a judgement call about evaluation economics, not a recitation of prices. When they ask how you debug a slow system, you show the latency profile and the before-and-after, and you explain why the refund-eligibility check dominated the ticket and what you did about it, which is a story about measurement leading to a fix, not a list of tools you have used. And when they ask how you know the system works, you show the benchmark table with its baselines and its caveats, and you explain what it does not cover, which is the answer that separates an experienced engineer from a candidate who has only ever run a demo: the experienced engineer knows the limits of their own evidence and says so without being asked. Bring the teardown to the review, walk the reviewers through it, and let them challenge it; a document that survives a hostile reading in public will survive a friendly one in a design review, and the reviewers will remember the engineer who brought evidence instead of adjectives.
