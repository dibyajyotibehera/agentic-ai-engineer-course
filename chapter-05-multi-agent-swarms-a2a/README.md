# Chapter 5 — Multi-Agent Swarms and Agent-to-Agent Handoffs

## The idea in plain words

A single agent is a person working alone at a desk: it reads the brief, thinks, uses its tools, and hands you an answer, and when the brief gets too big for one desk, the natural instinct is to hire more people. That instinct is where multi-agent systems begin, and it is also where most of them quietly go wrong, because adding agents does not add capability the way adding servers adds capacity. Each new agent brings its own reading of the task, its own partial view of the state, and its own chance to misunderstand what the others have already decided, so a swarm is not a bigger agent but a small organisation, and organisations fail in organisational ways: duplicated work, contradictory decisions, messages that get lost or garbled between desks, and nobody quite sure who is allowed to decide when two people disagree.

The chapter is therefore not about how to spawn ten agents; it is about how to keep ten agents honest. The discipline that makes a swarm work is the same discipline that makes a distributed system work: a shared contract for what a message means, a single owner for every decision, a bounded number of rounds before somebody has to stop and report, and a rule, written down before the run starts, for what happens when two agents disagree. Everything in this chapter follows from that one idea, and the running example will make it concrete: a support-ticket triage swarm that reads an incoming ticket, researches the account, checks policy, and drafts a reply, with a supervisor that plans the work, three worker agents that do it, and a set of guardrails that keep the whole run inside a budget of rounds, tokens and wall-clock time.

## Why a swarm at all, and why the answer is usually no

Before any mechanism, the question an experienced engineer has to answer is whether the swarm earns its complexity, and the honest answer, most of the time, is that it does not. Anthropic's engineers, writing up the multi-agent research system behind Claude Research, are unusually candid about the price: in their measurements, a single agent typically uses about four times as many tokens as a chat interaction, and a multi-agent system about fifteen times as many, which means a swarm has to buy its keep with a real improvement in outcome, not just a nicer diagram. On their internal research evaluation the multi-agent system outperformed a single agent by 90.2%, and that is a genuine result, but it was bought with fifteen times the tokens and it was won on broad, open-ended research questions where many independent directions could be pursued at once, which is exactly the shape of work that parallelises. The same post is explicit about where the pattern does not fit: domains where every agent must share the same context, or where the pieces depend on each other, and it notes that most coding tasks have fewer truly parallelisable subtasks than research does. Both numbers — the 90.2% gain and the 15× token cost — come from Anthropic's own internal evaluation, so treat them as the shape of the trade-off rather than a promise about your workload; the point that transfers is that the gain and the cost arrive together, and you have to measure both on your own tasks before you commit.

Cognition, the company behind the Devin coding agent, makes the counter-argument even more sharply in [Don't Build Multi-Agents](https://cognition.ai/blog/dont-build-multi-agents), and an experienced engineer should be able to state it fairly before disagreeing with it. Walden Yan's two principles are that agents should share context and share full agent traces, not just individual messages, and that actions carry implicit decisions, so conflicting decisions carry bad results. His worked example is two subagents asked to build the same game in parallel, one drawing a Super Mario-style background while the other builds a bird that matches nothing, and the coordinator is left trying to combine two miscommunications into one product. The failure is not a bug in either agent; it is structural, because neither agent could see the decisions the other had already made. Yan's later follow-up narrows the licence rather than withdrawing it: multi-agent systems work best today when writes stay single-threaded and the additional agents contribute intelligence rather than actions, which is why a reviewer agent that reads a diff and reports bugs is a good swarm member while a second coding agent editing the same files is usually a bad one. Hold both posts in your head at once and you have the real design rule for this chapter: parallelise reads and judgements, serialise writes, and never let two agents make the same implicit decision independently.

That rule is also the reason this chapter's running example is a triage swarm rather than a coding swarm. Triage is read-heavy: the researcher reads the account, the policy agent reads the rules, and the drafter writes exactly one artefact, the reply, under the supervisor's control. The writes are single-threaded by construction, the reads are parallel, and every disagreement is about a judgement — is this ticket urgent, does this policy apply — rather than about a shared mutable file. If your use case cannot be shaped that way, the swarm probably should not exist yet, and Chapter 1's single-agent loop with good tools is the right answer until the shape changes.

## The mechanism, part one: the Agent-to-Agent protocol and the agent card

Once the decision to build a swarm is made, the first engineering problem is that the agents need a way to find each other and talk, and if every pair of agents invents its own handshake, the system collapses into N-squared integrations the moment a fourth team ships an agent. The [Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol](https://a2a-protocol.org/latest/specification/) exists to prevent exactly that: it is an open standard, originally launched by Google Cloud in April 2025 and donated to the Linux Foundation, whose latest released version is 1.0.0, and it standardises how independent agents discover each other and exchange work without needing access to each other's internal state, memory or tools. That last clause matters more than it looks, because it is what makes A2A a protocol between organisations rather than a library inside one codebase: an agent built on one framework, in one language, by one team, can hand work to an agent built on another framework, in another language, by another team, and neither side has to trust the other's internals.

The protocol is organised in three layers, and the layering is the design lesson. Layer 1 is the canonical data model, defined in Protocol Buffers, with six core objects: Task, Message, AgentCard, Part, Artifact and Extension. Layer 2 is the abstract operations every implementation must support, such as sending a message, sending a streaming message, getting a task, listing tasks, cancelling a task and fetching an agent card. Layer 3 is the protocol bindings that map those operations onto the wire: JSON-RPC 2.0 over HTTP, gRPC, and HTTP+JSON in the REST style, with Server-Sent Events for streaming and webhooks for push notifications on long-running work. Because the data model is separate from the binding, a new transport can be added without changing what a task means, and because the operations are separate from the data model, two implementations can disagree about transport and still interoperate. If you have ever watched a team argue about whether the swarm should speak HTTP or gRPC before it has agreed on what a task is, this layering is the answer to that argument: agree on the objects first, pick a binding second, and let the binding change later without a rewrite.

Discovery in A2A starts with the agent card, which is a JSON document an agent publishes at a well-known URL, usually `/.well-known/agent-card.json`, describing its identity, its skills, its service endpoint and its authentication requirements. Think of it as the agent's business card and its service contract in one document: before you send it anything, you can read what it claims to do, what input shapes it accepts, and how you are expected to authenticate. The card is what makes dynamic composition possible, because a supervisor does not need to be compiled against a worker's code; it needs to read the worker's card, check that the skill it needs is listed, and send a task. It is also the first trust boundary in the system, and Chapter 7 will return to what happens when a card lies, but for this chapter the point is architectural: capability discovery is a runtime read, not a build-time import, which is what lets a swarm grow by adding agents rather than by redeploying the supervisor.

A task is the protocol's fundamental unit of work, and it has a defined lifecycle rather than a single request-response shape. A client sends a message and receives either a direct response message, for simple interactions that need no tracking, or a task object that tracks the processing asynchronously, and the task then moves through states that include working, input-required, completed, failed, cancelled and rejected. That lifecycle is what lets a handoff survive the realities of production: a worker can ask for more input halfway through, a supervisor can poll or subscribe to progress, a long-running task can outlive a single HTTP request through streaming or push notifications, and a cancelled task is a first-class outcome rather than a connection that somebody closed. The execution modes follow from the same thinking: synchronous for quick work, asynchronous for long work, streaming for work whose intermediate results matter, and push notifications for work that outlives the caller's attention. A swarm that only supports synchronous calls will either time out on real work or force every worker to pretend its work is quick, so the lifecycle and the modes are not protocol trivia; they are the reason a handoff can be durable.

A2A sits next to, not instead of, the [Model Context Protocol (MCP)](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/basic), and confusing the two is the most common category error in multi-agent design. MCP connects an agent to its tools and data; A2A connects an agent to other agents. A remote A2A agent may well use MCP internally to do the work it was asked to do, but the protocols answer different questions, and a system that tries to use one for the other's job ends up either exposing its tools to strangers or trying to express a delegation as a tool call. Keep the boundary clean: tools are capabilities an agent owns, agents are peers an agent delegates to, and the handoff between peers goes through A2A.

Google's [Agent Development Kit (ADK)](https://developers.googleblog.com/build-cross-language-multi-agent-team-with-google-agent-development-kit-and-a2a/) is the reference implementation this chapter points to, because it shows both directions of the pattern in a few lines of code: `to_a2a` turns an ADK agent into an A2A server that publishes an agent card, and `RemoteA2aAgent` wraps a remote A2A agent so it can be used as a sub-agent inside an ADK workflow. The cross-language example in Google's own write-up — a Python agent extracting contract terms and a Go agent validating them against policy, connected only by A2A — is the shape to internalise: neither agent imports the other's code, neither knows the other's framework, and the only shared surface is the protocol. [IBM's BeeAI framework](https://github.com/i-am-bee/beeai-framework) plays the same role from the IBM side, with an `A2AServer` adapter for exposing agents and an `A2AAgent` adapter for consuming them, and it is the framework the DeepLearning.AI course uses for its final orchestration lesson. You do not need to adopt either framework to use the ideas, and the code in this chapter is deliberately framework-neutral, but reading one real implementation is worth more than reading ten diagrams, because it shows you where the protocol's abstractions leak in practice.

## The mechanism, part two: typed state passing, or how handoffs stop corrupting state

A protocol tells agents how to exchange messages; it does not tell them what the messages mean, and that gap is where swarms corrupt state. The failure looks innocent: the researcher agent returns a summary as free text, the supervisor pastes that text into the drafter's prompt, the drafter misreads a date or invents a missing field, and the customer receives a reply that confidently states the wrong renewal date. Nothing crashed, no exception was raised, and the corruption is invisible until a human reads the output, which is precisely why it is dangerous. The fix is typed state passing: every handoff carries a payload whose shape is declared in advance, validated on receipt, and versioned so that a sender and a receiver running different versions of the schema can detect the mismatch instead of silently misreading each other.

In practice that means three things, and the code in this chapter implements all three. First, the state that crosses a handoff is a schema, not a string: a Pydantic model or a dataclass with named fields, types, required versus optional markers, and constraints such as "priority is one of low, medium, high or urgent" rather than a free-text word that might arrive as "pretty urgent". Second, the receiver validates before it acts, and validation failure is a handled outcome with its own path — reject the payload, log the exact field that failed, and either ask the sender to retry with a corrected payload or escalate to the supervisor — never a silent coercion where a bad value is guessed at and the run continues. Third, the schema carries a version, and the receiver knows which versions it accepts, so a worker deployed yesterday and a supervisor deployed today can negotiate rather than corrupt. The A2A protocol itself gives you the envelope — messages made of parts, artefacts made of parts, structured data carried as a part type — but the schema inside the part is your design decision, and it is the decision that determines whether your swarm degrades gracefully or corrupts quietly.

There is a second, subtler reason typed state matters, which is that it bounds what a worker can smuggle into the next agent's context. When handoffs are free text, every worker's entire reasoning trace, including its mistakes, its hallucinations and its irrelevant asides, flows downstream and lands in the next agent's prompt, where it costs tokens and, worse, looks like evidence. Anthropic's research system learned this the hard way and converged on a discipline this chapter adopts wholesale: subagents return condensed, distilled summaries rather than raw traces, and the supervisor's context stays small because it only ever sees the distilled result. Typed state enforces that discipline mechanically, because a schema with five fields cannot carry a three-thousand-token ramble, and the act of filling in the fields forces the worker to decide what it actually concluded. The schema is therefore doing two jobs at once: it is a contract that catches corruption, and it is a compression step that keeps the swarm's token bill inside the budget.

The code makes this concrete with a `TaskEnvelope` that wraps every handoff: a task identifier, a schema version, the sender, the intended recipient, the typed payload, and a checksum over the payload bytes. The checksum is deliberately old-fashioned, and deliberately there: it catches the class of failure where the payload is well-formed but not the payload that was sent, whether because a transport mangled it, a retry delivered a stale copy, or a worker edited a field after signing. The receiver recomputes the checksum, compares it against the envelope, and rejects the handoff on mismatch, which turns a silent corruption into a loud, logged, recoverable error. That is the theme of the whole chapter in miniature: every failure mode you can name in advance becomes a check, and every check turns a silent degradation into a visible one.

## The mechanism, part three: supervisor topologies and their trade-offs

With the protocol and the state contract in place, the remaining design question is topology: who talks to whom, and who decides. There are four shapes worth knowing, and the choice between them is the highest-leverage decision in a swarm design, because topology determines where failures concentrate, where the token bill lands, and how hard the system is to debug.

The supervisor topology is the workhorse, and it is the one this chapter builds. One central agent receives the task, decides which worker to call, reads the worker's result, and decides again, until it can answer or until it hits a bound. Workers never talk to each other; every message passes through the supervisor, which means the supervisor is the only agent that holds the whole picture, the only place routing decisions are made, and the only place the run can be terminated. LangGraph documents this as its supervisor architecture, with a tool-calling variant in which each worker is exposed to the supervisor as a tool, so the supervisor's routing decision is literally a tool call with typed arguments — a neat convergence with the typed state passing above, because the tool's input schema is the handoff contract. The supervisor's strengths are legibility and control: there is one place to read the plan, one place to add a guardrail, one place to stop the run, and one agent whose prompt you can improve when routing goes wrong. Its weaknesses are equally clear: the supervisor is a bottleneck and a single point of failure, its context grows with every worker result it reads, and if its routing judgement is bad, no worker can route around it. Anthropic's research system is a supervisor topology with the bottleneck managed deliberately: the lead agent plans, spawns three to five subagents in parallel, and each subagent returns a condensed summary, so the supervisor's context stays small and its job stays coordination rather than comprehension.

The peer-to-peer swarm, which LangGraph implements as a swarm with handoff tools, removes the centre: any agent can hand control to any other agent, and the system remembers which agent was last active so a conversation resumes with it. This is the right shape when the next step genuinely cannot be predicted in advance and depends on what the current agent just learned — a support conversation that might need billing, then engineering, then billing again, with the handoff decided by the agent holding the conversation rather than by a distant coordinator. Its cost is that there is no single place where the run's state lives, no single agent that can see the whole trajectory, and no natural place to enforce a global budget, so a peer-to-peer swarm needs explicit machinery — a shared state object with a hop counter, a visited set, and a termination rule — or it will wander. The rule of thumb is that if you cannot draw the handoff graph on a whiteboard and point to where it ends, you have built a peer-to-peer swarm by accident, and you should either add a supervisor or add the termination machinery before you ship it.

The hierarchical topology is a supervisor of supervisors: a top-level orchestrator delegates to sub-supervisors, each of which manages its own team. It earns its keep when the swarm is large enough that one supervisor's context cannot hold the whole plan, or when teams are genuinely independent — one team per product area, one per region, one per customer tier — and the top level only needs to know which team owns a request. Its cost is latency and compounding error: every layer adds a round trip and another chance for a summary to lose the detail that mattered, so the hierarchy should be as shallow as the problem allows, and each layer should add a decision that the layer below cannot make, not merely forward messages. Two layers is usually enough; three layers is a smell that the decomposition is wrong.

The fourth shape is the parallel fan-out with aggregation, which is less a topology than a pattern that sits inside any of the others: the same task, or several independent slices of it, is sent to multiple workers at once, and their results are combined by a fixed rule in code rather than by another model call. Anthropic's parallelisation pattern names its two variants, sectioning, where the subtasks are independent, and voting, where the same task is run several times and the results are compared, and the distinction matters because the aggregation rule is different: sectioning merges disjoint results, voting resolves disagreement, and the next section covers how. Fan-out is where the swarm's speed comes from — Anthropic reports that parallel subagents cut research time by up to 90% on complex queries — but it is also where the token bill multiplies fastest, because every worker runs the full task, so the fan-out factor should be a configuration value with a default, not a number the model chooses freely.

The decision between these shapes is a design review question, not a coding question, and the review should ask four things in order: is the work decomposable into pieces with clean boundaries, or does every piece depend on choices made in the others; is the output a single artefact with one writer, or are several agents writing to shared state; can the routing decision be made by a fixed rule in code, or does it need judgement on every run; and is there a natural owner for the final answer. If the work is not decomposable, build one agent. If several agents must write to shared state, serialise the writes or build one agent. If routing is a fixed rule, write the rule in code and skip the supervisor model call. Only when the work decomposes, the writes are single-threaded, and the routing needs judgement does the supervisor earn its place, and only when the routing needs judgement from the agent holding the conversation, rather than from a coordinator, does the peer-to-peer swarm earn its.

### Subagents versus agent teams: a cost and coordination choice

Within any topology there is a second choice that is easy to miss, because both shapes look like "more agents": whether the extra agents are subagents or a team. A subagent is spawned by a central coordinator for one narrow brief, lives only for that job, and returns a condensed summary rather than its full trace — the coordinator owns the run's context, and the subagent's context is small and disposable, which is why its cost stays bounded by the brief it was given. That is the shape Anthropic's research system uses, and it is the shape this chapter's triage swarm uses too: the researcher and the policy checker are subagents in this sense, each doing one read or judgement and handing back a typed result.

An agent team is the opposite arrangement: peer-to-peer agents that each hold their own persistent context, live for the whole conversation, and negotiate the next step among themselves, so nobody has to be told what to do next — but everybody has to be kept up to date, which is why the cost grows on two fronts at once, every member carrying context and coordination messages multiplying between them. The peer-to-peer swarm described above is a team in this sense, and its strength is exactly what its cost buys: when the next step genuinely cannot be predicted in advance and depends on what the agent holding the conversation just learned, a team can follow the conversation where it leads without waiting for a coordinator to re-plan.

So the choice follows the work, and it follows the chapter's design rule rather than fighting it. Choose subagents when the work decomposes into independent read or judgement slices with one writer, because then the coordinator can hand out narrow briefs, collect condensed summaries, and keep the writes single-threaded — that is the triage swarm, and the supervisor topology is the natural home for it. Choose a team when the handoff cannot be planned in advance — the support conversation that needs billing, then engineering, then billing again is this chapter's own example — and the coordination cost is worth paying because no coordinator could have written the route in advance. As a cost intuition, not a measured number: a team of N agents each holding context costs roughly N times the context per round plus the coordination messages between them, while a subagent's cost is bounded by its brief. The Anthropic multi-agent token figure quoted earlier in this chapter is the measured version of that intuition, so treat the intuition as a way to reason about the trade-off and the measured figure as the evidence that the trade-off is real. In an interview, answer this choice the same way: name who owns the context, say how long each agent lives, and say what bounds the cost — and if you cannot name the bound, you have not chosen a topology, you have only drawn one. | | Subagents | Agent teams |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the context | The coordinator; the subagent's context is small and disposable | Each member holds its own persistent context |
| Lifetime | Short-lived, one narrow brief | Long-lived, for the whole conversation |
| Coordination | Through the coordinator; no member-to-member messages | Peer negotiation; messages multiply between members |
| Cost shape | Bounded by the brief | Roughly N times the context per round, plus coordination messages (intuition, not a measured number) |
| Fits when | Independent read or judgement slices with one writer | The next step is unpredictable and depends on what the current agent just learned |

## The mechanism, part four: consensus voting and conflict resolution

Sooner or later two workers will disagree, and the swarm needs a rule for that moment that was written before the run started, because a rule invented mid-run by a language model is not a rule; it is a guess wearing a rule's clothes. Consensus voting is the simplest such rule, and it is worth understanding precisely because it is simple: run the same judgement through several independent workers, collect their structured verdicts, and apply a deterministic aggregation function to decide. The independence is the load-bearing part, and it has to be engineered rather than assumed: workers that share a prompt, a model, a context window or a retrieved evidence set are not independent voters, they are the same voter counted several times, and their agreement tells you nothing. Give each voter its own slice of the evidence, or its own framing of the question, or at minimum its own fresh context, and only then does a majority mean something.

The aggregation rule should be chosen for the stakes of the decision, and there are four worth having in your toolkit. Majority vote is the default: three voters, two agree, the third is outvoted, and the run continues with the majority's answer and a note that a dissent exists. It is cheap, legible, and wrong in exactly one known way, which is that it discards the minority, so it should only be used where the minority's information is genuinely redundant rather than complementary. Weighted vote is the refinement: voters carry weights derived from something measurable, such as a track record on a held-out evaluation set, a confidence score the voter itself reports, or the quality of the evidence it cited, and the weights are set by configuration rather than by the model. Unanimity is the rule for irreversible actions: if the swarm is about to refund a customer, delete data, or send an external message, require every voter to agree, and escalate to a human on any dissent, because the cost of a wrong irreversible action dwarfs the cost of a slow one. And escalation itself is the fourth rule and the most important: when the vote is tied, when the voters disagree about a fact rather than a judgement, or when the disagreement reveals that the task was underspecified, the correct outcome is not a vote but a stop — the supervisor records the disagreement, with each voter's evidence attached, and routes it to a human or to a more capable single agent with the full context. A swarm that cannot say "we disagree and we do not know" will instead manufacture a confident wrong answer, which is the worst possible outcome.

There is one more distinction that separates a serious consensus design from a demo, which is the difference between disagreeing about a fact and disagreeing about a judgement. If two workers return different renewal dates for the same account, that is a factual disagreement, and no amount of voting resolves it, because the truth is not decided by ballot; the correct response is to re-fetch the source of truth and let the data decide, and if the sources themselves disagree, to escalate. If two workers return different priorities for the same ticket, that is a judgement disagreement, and voting is a legitimate way to resolve it, because priority is a decision rather than a fact. The supervisor therefore needs to know, per field, whether the field is a fact with a source of truth or a judgement with a resolution rule, and the typed state from the previous section is where that knowledge lives: the schema marks which fields are verifiable against a source and which are verdicts, and the conflict-resolution rule consults the schema before it consults the votes. That is the difference between a swarm that argues and a swarm that resolves.

## The failure modes, and how to bound them

Every failure mode in this section is one you will meet in production if you do not design against it, and the bounding strategy is the same in each case: turn the failure into a number with a limit, check the number on every round, and stop the run when the limit is reached. A swarm without limits is not a system; it is a loop with a credit card attached.

The first failure mode is runaway fan-out, and Anthropic's post gives the canonical example: early versions of their research system spawned fifty subagents for simple queries, because nothing in the system knew how much effort a query deserved. The bound is an effort ladder written in configuration, not in prose: a fact-finding question gets one agent and a small tool-call budget, a direct comparison gets two to four subagents, and only a genuinely complex research question gets more than ten, with each tier carrying explicit limits on subagents and on tool calls per subagent. The supervisor's prompt should contain the ladder, but the enforcement should live in code, because a model that is asked to judge effort will over-invest in the queries it finds interesting and under-invest in the ones it finds boring, which is exactly backwards. The code in this chapter enforces a maximum fan-out of three workers for the triage swarm, a maximum of three supervisor rounds, a per-worker token budget, and a wall-clock deadline, and any of those limits ending the run is a normal outcome that produces a partial result with a clear status, not an exception.

The second failure mode is the telephone game, where each handoff loses a little information and adds a little invention, so the final answer is confidently wrong about a detail that was correct at the start. The mechanism is mundane: a worker summarises a source, the supervisor summarises the summary, the drafter writes from the supervisor's summary, and at each step a qualifier drops out — "the customer is on the annual plan, renewing in March" becomes "the customer renews in March" becomes "the customer's renewal is due" — until the reply asserts something nobody verified. The bounds are typed state passing, which forces each handoff to carry the actual values rather than a paraphrase of them, and a provenance rule: every factual claim in the final artefact must carry the identifier of the worker and the source it came from, so a claim that cannot point at its source is dropped rather than repeated. The code implements provenance as a field on every worker result, and the supervisor's synthesis step refuses to include a factual claim whose provenance is missing.

The third failure mode is the routing loop, where the supervisor sends a task to a worker, the worker returns a result the supervisor does not accept, the supervisor sends a slightly different version of the same task to the same worker, and the run burns its budget going around in circles. The bound is a visited set and a round counter: the supervisor records every routing decision it has made, refuses to route the same task to the same worker twice with the same brief, and stops after a fixed number of rounds with whatever it has. The fourth failure mode is the silent worker, where a worker hangs, crashes, or returns a malformed result, and the supervisor waits forever or proceeds as if the worker had succeeded. The bound is a per-worker timeout with a defined fallback — retry once with a simpler brief, then proceed without that worker's contribution and mark the result partial — because a swarm that cannot complete without every worker is a swarm with the availability of its least reliable member. The fifth failure mode is the chatter spiral, where agents spend their budget talking to each other rather than doing the work: status updates, acknowledgements, clarifying questions that clarify nothing, and coordination messages that multiply with the square of the agent count. The bound is a message budget alongside the token budget, and a design rule that workers do not message each other at all in a supervisor topology, so the only conversation in the system is the one the supervisor controls.

Underneath all five is the failure mode that matters most, which is the absence of observability. A single agent's trajectory is already hard to debug; a swarm's trajectory is a set of interleaved trajectories, and without a trace that records every handoff — who sent what to whom, in which round, with which payload checksum, and what came back — a failure is undiagnosable. The code therefore logs every handoff as a structured event with a run identifier, a round number, a sender, a recipient, a payload schema version, a checksum, and a latency, and the exercises ask you to read a trace and find the fault in it, because that is the skill you will actually use at 2 a.m. Chapter 10 will build the evaluation harness that grades these trajectories; this chapter's job is to make sure the trajectories exist to be graded.

## The advanced-level interview angle

An interviewer at an advanced level is not testing whether you can name the A2A protocol; they are testing whether you can say no to a swarm, and then, when the swarm is justified, whether you can bound it. The strongest answer to "design a multi-agent system for X" starts by questioning the premise: ask what decomposes, ask who writes, ask what the single-agent baseline achieves, and ask what the token budget is. Then, if the case survives, describe the system in the order this chapter used: the contract first, the topology second, the resolution rules third, and the bounds last. Name the protocol and the agent card when the agents cross a trust or team boundary, and say plainly when they do not — agents inside one codebase, owned by one team, deployed together, do not need A2A, and reaching for it there is architecture astronautics. Describe state passing as typed schemas with validation and versioning, not as prompts passed between models, and be ready to sketch the schema for the handoff in the question. Describe the topology by its failure concentration: the supervisor is a bottleneck you accept for legibility, the peer-to-peer swarm is a graph you must be able to draw and terminate, the hierarchy is a latency cost you pay only for genuine independence. Describe conflict resolution as a rule chosen for the stakes — majority for cheap judgements, unanimity for irreversible actions, escalation for factual disagreement — and say what happens to the dissenting evidence, because a system that throws the minority away is a system that will repeat its mistakes. And finish with the bounds, because that is where seniority shows: maximum fan-out, maximum rounds, per-worker timeouts, a token budget with a kill-switch, and a trace that records every handoff, all enforced in code rather than requested in prose. If you can also quote the shape of the cost — that multi-agent systems run an order of magnitude more tokens than a single-agent call, and that the gain has to be measured on the team's own evaluation rather than assumed from a vendor's blog post — you have answered the question the way an experienced engineer answers it, which is by showing where the system can fail and what you did about it before it did.
